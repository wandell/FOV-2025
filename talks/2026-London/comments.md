# Review of 2026 London Talk Draft (index.qmd)

First, the overarching narrative is excellent. The structural parallel between the two halves is very satisfying: Half 1 deconstructs a classic model (the Felleman and Van Essen hierarchy) and replaces it with a modern meso-scale framework (Clusters); Half 2 deconstructs a classic 1990s dogma (massive adult cortical plasticity) and replaces it with a modern mechanistic framework (stability via Perineuronal Nets). 

However, presenting this in **24 minutes (12 minutes per topic)** will be incredibly tight. Right now, the document reads perfectly for a book chapter (which you noted it will become), but for a short talk, the historical context and literature reviews will swallow your time budget.

Here are my thoughts on how to tighten the organization for a 12-minute limit per section:

## Part 1: Visual Field Maps & Clusters (12 mins)

*   **The Overcrowded History:** You currently spend a lot of real estate on the Ferrier/Munk debates, Henschen, and Holmes/Inouye. While fascinating, it might eat up 4–5 minutes of your 12. 
    *   *Recommendation:* Compress this to a single introductory slide ("From early clinical lesions to V1/V2"). Jump straight to the Felleman & Van Essen (1991) diagram to establish the baseline of what everyone *thinks* visual cortex looks like. 
*   **The Critique of "Areas":** The FACT criteria and the critique of the term "area" is a slight tangent. It's conceptually important, but if you need to save time, you can simply state: "We relied on macaque 'areas' for too long, but human fMRI shows a different fundamental unit: the cluster."
*   **The Main Arc:** Keep this strictly linear. 
    1. The Classic View (Felleman & Van Essen).
    2. The fMRI Revolution (Eccentricity/Angle maps -> finding the secondary fovea).
    3. The Discovery and Concept of Clusters (Brewer).
    4. Why Humans $\neq$ Macaques (Comparative anatomy, hV4).
    5. The New Diagram (Zeki / Grill-Spector / Clusters).

## Part 2: Adult Cortical Plasticity (12 mins)

*   **Developmental vs. Adult:** Your thesis here is the stability of the *adult* cortex. However, you spend significant time on Achiasmia, Albinism, and Rod Monochromats (Congenital Achromatopsia). Because these are *developmental* conditions, they might dilute your central argument about *adult* plasticity.
    *   *Recommendation:* If your focus is adult stability, prioritize the acquired retinal diseases (Macular Degeneration, Retinitis Pigmentosa) and your Macaque binocular lesion work (Smirnakis). You might mention developmental congenital cases merely as a footnote about how deeply baked-in the topographic blueprint is, but keep the spotlight on adult lesions.
*   **The Molecular Brake:** The pivot to Perineuronal Nets (PNNs) and Takao Hensch's work is a fantastic closer. It provides the "Why?" to the systems-level "What?". 
    *   *Recommendation:* Dedicate the final 3-4 minutes to this mechanism. It bridges the gap between systems neuroscience and cellular biology perfectly, explaining *why* the 1990s hype around adult plasticity failed to materialize in sensory cortices.

## Summary
The scientific narrative is incredibly strong, but you have about 45 minutes of material here. To hit 12 minutes apiece:
1. Shorten the late 19th and early 20th-century history.
2. Cut the developmental/congenital plasticity examples in favor of acquired adult lesions.
3. Make the two revised "diagrams" the heroes of the talk: the new Cluster-based functional map, and the PNN-locked cortical circuit that locks / PNN mechanisms that keep it stable.
